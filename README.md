# Revelry & Valor: The Game of Legends — World Simulation

A world simulator for writers, dungeon masters and anyone who wants to watch an imagined world come to life. Generate a planet, seed it with peoples, and watch centuries of history unfold: tribes settle the land, farm and trade, migrate, invent bronze and writing, splinter into new cultures, forge kingdoms and empires, and go to war. Nothing is scripted. Every event comes from the world's geography and resources and from the choices of its peoples.

Every run is deterministic. The same seed and settings always produce the same history, so a world you like can be shared or regenerated.

## Quick start

```bash
npm install
npm run dev        # open the interactive map in your browser
npm run sim -- --seed 42 --years 600 --out chronicle.md   # headless run, writes a full chronicle
npm test
```

CLI options: `--seed`, `--years`, `--size small|medium|large|huge`, `--magic 0|1|2`, `--name`, `--importance 1|2|3` (how much of the chronicle to print), `--out file.md`.

## Using the map

- **Play** runs the world in **real time**, month by month, through the seasons (the top bar shows the year, month and season). Settlers trek to new land, armies muster in spring, march, fight and besiege, and caravans and convoys travel their routes, all moving smoothly between months. The speed slider sets months per second (0.2 to 12).
- **+1 / +10 / +100 / +500** skip ahead whole years whenever you like. Turn **Real time** off to have Play advance in whole years instead (the slider then sets years per second).
- **Map style:** **Drawn map** (the default), **Parchment** or **Satellite**, chosen in the map bar. The styles differ only in colouring, so switching costs nothing.
  - *Drawn map* looks like a coloured cartographer's map drawn on paper:
    - soft watercolour washes for each kind of ground on cream paper, browning with height;
    - snow where it is cold enough;
    - an inked coastline and lake shores, with blue seas and ripple lines along the shore;
    - relief lit from the north-west. The sunlit side stays close to the paper and the shadow side is laid on in a warm grey wash.

    **Mountains are carved by running water**, the way terrain programs like Gaea and Wilbur erode a heightmap (the workflow behind Kilroy's Kartography's realistic fantasy maps). The land is laid out on a grid four times finer than the map's tiles, and the high ground is roughened so the water has something to bite on. Then rain runs downhill to the sea or a lake, gathers into streams, and each stream cuts down by how much water it carries and how steep it runs. Big valleys are carved first, then finer gullies. What stands between the valleys is a sharp crest with spurs running down to the valley floor, and valleys branch up into the ranges as in real mountains. Coasts and lakes stay where they are. Every style is shaded from this carved land. It is carved once per world (about a second), and the shape of the land itself still comes from the world generator or the terrain tool.
  - *Parchment* draws the same land in the colours of an old map: paper, an inked coastline and lake shores, ripple lines along the shore, soft sepia relief, and forests hatched in fine diagonal ink lines.
  - *Satellite* paints the land as seen from orbit: ground coloured by what covers it, bare rock on steep slopes and above the tree line, snow, and turquoise shallows darkening to deep ocean.
- **Map layers:** Terrain (rivers drawn as crisp winding lines that widen as they gather water; close in, the visible part is repainted at full screen resolution once the view settles), Realms (political borders), Nations (solid map-style fills with bold borders and each nation's name written across its lands), Cultures, Races (all four drawn as smooth shapes with natural, wandering borders that stop at the painted coastline), **Temperature** (yearly mean), **Rainfall**, **Currents** (arrows for the way the surface water flows; red where it runs warmer than usual for its latitude, blue where colder), and Resources (pick iron, tin, gold, ley lines and others). What each settlement does with its land shows in its Inspect panel, not on the map. Selecting a nation, or any settlement in it, dims the rest of the world, traces the nation's borders in a bright smooth outline and writes its name large. Nations bound to it by marriage, alliance, confederation or vassalage stay half-lit with dashed borders in their own colour. Towns cut off from their capital wear a red ring. Esc clears the selection. **Roads** are drawn by level: dotted trails, brown cart tracks, grey paved roads and dark highways. **Trade routes** (dashed) show only where free traders (gold) and state convoys (green) actually travel. Caravans are coloured by kind: gold merchants, purple trading families, orange nomad caravan tribes, and green squares for convoys. A diamond ◆ marks a capital, a triangle ▲ is an army on the march (its dashed line shows where it is heading), a white triangle △ is a column of settlers (its faint trail leads to the land they mean to settle), and gold dots ● are merchant caravans. A thick ring in an enemy's colour marks an occupied town.
- **Market:** the market of the selected town. For each good it shows what the town holds and needs, what the good is worth there compared with the average market, and how the value has moved since last year. With no town selected, it shows the world market: where each good is cheapest and dearest, and how much trade is paid in coin versus barter.
- **Nation view:** a full dossier on any nation. It shows its court (the ruling dynasty and every great house with its seat, fiefs, prestige and loyalty, flagging houses that are plotting), its diplomacy (marriages, alliances, confederation, overlord or vassals, war tribute, towns it claims, towns cut off), what the nation makes, what it needs and what it is short of, its internal, caravan and convoy trade, whether it uses coin or barter, its agreements, how it treats other nations' traders (and how they treat its own), embargoes, trading families, roads by level, tariff, armies and wars, research progress, peoples and cultures (with their survival traits), rulers, settlements, population history and chronicle.
- **Tech tree:** every technology by era, with prerequisite paths drawn between them. Pick a nation to see what it knows, what it is learning and how far along it is (several at once, each through its own practice), what it can research next, and what it is blocked from by a missing resource. Select a tech to light up the path leading to it and everything it unlocks, and to see which realms know it and who discovered it first.
- **Inspect:** click any settlement, realm or piece of land. Settlements show their size, what they are built of, how many craftsmen their size supports, their town (townsfolk and City land), how crowded they are, how many tiles of land their ring gives them and what they use them for, population by race, food, stability and loyalty, their food stores (how many months they last, how much of the year's harvest is still to come, and how many lean months they had), whether they are under enemy occupation, which noble house holds them, who claims them, and whether they are cut off or surrounded, their workforce trade by trade (farmers, herders, hunters and gatherers, fishers, woodcutters, quarrymen, miners, salt workers, toolsmiths, weaponsmiths, artisans, and those without work, each with a headcount), which market they trade through (or which settlements come to their market), what they sell, buy and only pass on, the tribute they send or receive, last year's accounts good by good (made, used, bought, sold, passed on), trade partners, great works and local history. Realms show their ruler and dynasty, neighbours and relations, known technology, wars and population over time.
- **Chronicle:** the full history, filterable by importance, kind and name. Click an entry to jump to it on the map. Export it as Markdown for your notes, or download the world as JSON.
- **Setup:** seed, map size, land share, temperature, rainfall, the **latitudes of the map's top and bottom edges** (from a pole-to-pole world to a single region, such as 20°N to 60°N), **axial tilt**, **ocean currents** on or off, mineral wealth, magic level, calamity frequency, settlement spacing, and a JSON editor for the **races**. Change their biome preferences, growth, lifespan, strengths, research talents, values, and the sound palette their names are built from, or add entirely new peoples.

## What is simulated

| System | How it works |
|---|---|
| **Terrain & climate** | Continents with ridged mountain ranges, or land shaped by hand in the world editor. Temperature follows latitude (the map can sit anywhere on the globe), axial tilt and altitude. Wind-driven ocean currents carry warm and cold water along the coasts. Prevailing winds carry rain inland, leaving rain shadows and subtropical deserts. Rivers form from drainage (priority-flood), with lakes in wet basins and salt flats in dry ones. |
| **Resources** | Soil fertility, timber, game, fish, stone, copper, tin, iron, coal, gold, gems, salt, wild horses and ley lines. Ore deposits follow geology: mountains and hills carry most ores, rivers carry placer gold, and tin is deliberately scarce. |
| **Clans, tribes and peoples** | By default every people begins as a few **clans** of wandering **tribes** (Setup can start them as settled villages instead). A tribe of 30 to 150 people follows its food through the seasons: summer grounds in spring, sheltered winter grounds (woods, river valleys, low ground) in autumn, and on again early when the land runs thin. It hunts, gathers and fishes the wild land around its camp, or follows its herds on grassland and steppe once animals are tamed, and the land it uses wears out for a while. **Hunting grounds:** a new tribe roams for a few years until it finds land rich enough in game and wild food, then makes it its **home range**. From then on it **claims land by walking it**: the open land it passes through and camps on during its seasonal round becomes its clan's, as long as it lies next to land the clan already holds, until the clan's land around home can feed the tribe with room to spare. Because only a small share of what wild land holds can be taken year after year, hunters need far more land than farmers. A tribe that outgrows its range splits: the new tribe goes looking for land of its own at the edge of the clan's grounds and claims more, and in a large clan some break away under a chief of their own as a new clan. Land never lapses. Unclaimed land stays unclaimed until someone walks into it, and land someone holds changes hands only by war. **Clan wars:** a tribe that needs more land, with no open land left around it and a neighbour holding what it needs, may **declare war** (hungry, warlike or feuding clans most readily). It sends out a **warband**, the fighting men and women of the clan's tribes nearby. The warband marches on the map, occupying the enemy's land as it goes. The defenders gather a warband of their own to meet it. Warbands fight when they meet, the stronger usually winning (weapons, horses, warlike ways, home ground and hills count), and the loser goes home. Warbands go home after a campaign, and the survivors return to their tribes, so every warrior lost is a person lost. When the fighting is done, the side that won more of it wins the war. Land the winner holds by force becomes its own, and land the loser took goes back. A truce follows, and clans feud. Tribes whose home range is lost go looking for new land. Clans fight nations the same way: a clan can take a nation's land, and a growing settlement hemmed in by a clan's hunting grounds may declare war to clear them, sending a warband of its own people. Clans learn as nations do, through practice (gatherers find agriculture, hunters tame animals, fishers take to boats) and much faster from settled neighbours. Tribes meet settled folk: they trade meat, hides and horses for grain and tools, raid them when hungry, or settle among them. Each race has a **lifestyle**: *settled* peoples settle as soon as farming (or, more rarely, a rich fishing ground) makes it pay, *semi-nomadic* ones take longer, *nomadic* ones hardly ever unless boxed in by settled lands. A clan's first settlement makes it a **nation**; its other tribes may follow. On the map a tribe is a small tent in its clan's colour (with a pale dot when it herds), with a faint trail to its next ground, and each clan's hunting grounds are a pale wash in its colour on the Realms, Nations, Cultures and Races layers. Click a tribe to see its clan, home range, the clan's land, its fights and feuds, and how it is faring. Click land to see whose hunting grounds it is. The Peoples tab shows each culture's settled and wandering people, clans and tribes. |
| **Land** | Every settlement sits on a single tile whatever its size, and works the **ring of land** around it: every tile within half the settlement spacing (2 tiles with the default spacing of 4). Where two rings overlap, each tile goes to the nearer settlement, so neighbours split the land between them; the sea, mountains and ice leave a settlement less to work. Each tile is put to one use, chosen by what the settlement needs and what the ground allows (mines only on ore, quarries on stone, salt works on salt, fishing on water): fields, pasture, forestry, hunting grounds, mine, quarry, salt works or fishing waters. A new settlement shares its land out between uses, food first, keeping a mix rather than putting everything to one use. After that people keep to what they know: a tile changes use only when the new use is clearly worth more, and only a tile or two a year, more in hungry times. Clearing woods for fields brings in their timber. A settlement that grows bigger than its land can feed buys its food from its neighbours. The ring is never drawn: it only tells a settlement what land and resources it has to work with. What the map shows as a nation's territory is its **realm**: every tile falls to the nearest settlement within three times the ring's reach (and coastal waters within the ring), so nations appear as continuous lands with borders only between nations. Realms decide borders, tolls and passage for traders, where armies fight on home ground, and which towns are cut off. |
| **Development** | A settlement's size comes from its people alone: camp, hamlet, village, town (2,000), city (8,000), great city (30,000), metropolis (100,000). Bigger places keep more specialists busy: a camp can spare 3% of its workers for crafts, a village 10%, a town 20%, a city a third, a metropolis half. From town size on, a settlement has a **town at its heart**: its craftsmen, merchants and officials and their families live there, and as they outgrow it the town is built over more of its land: its own tile first, then whatever land it needs least, which is never farmed again. Some peoples build wide and low, others pack tight. Technology never stops a settlement growing: building skills, water and sanitation only decide how many it holds **in comfort**. Beyond that it is **crowded**: more people die of filth and sickness, plague breaks out more often, stability falls, people leave, and great **fires** sweep towns of timber and tents (much less often towns of stone or mud brick). What a settlement is built of comes from its land and its people: hide tents for herders and hunters, timber where there is wood, mud brick on dry plains, stone once masonry is known and stone is to hand. People without work drift away to where there is some, and few move to a town full of idle hands. |
| **Settlements & economy** | Each settlement works the tiles of its ring. Each year it works out what its people would ideally do (enough food first, then whatever pays best, here or at the markets it trades with after carriage, so mining towns dig ore for the smiths around them and farming villages grow grain for the cities they feed, with diminishing returns as resources are worked harder), but **people keep to their trades**: the workforce only moves towards that as fast as need drives it. Hunger sends up to a third of the workforce to the fields in a year; a strong demand draws in a few percent a year; a glut does not make anyone give up their craft at once (the price falls and traders come for the cheap goods), and workers drift away only slowly, quickly only from work that has vanished altogether. The crafts grow only as fast as masters can train apprentices. Whoever finds no place is without work. Crafts turn stone, copper, bronze, iron or steel into tools and weapons; gold and gems become luxuries. Homes need timber, and stone once masonry is known. Food spoils unless stored or salted. |
| **Seasons** | Time runs month by month. Food comes in by season: farmers bring in the grain at harvest (Harvest and Leaffall), hunters and gatherers find most from spring to autumn, and herds and fish feed people all year, leaner in winter. People eat from the stores every month, and food slowly rots unless it is stored or salted, so a poor harvest means hungry months before the next one. Births and deaths happen month by month. Settlers wait out the winter and set off in spring, armies muster in spring and march more slowly through winter (and suffer more from it), and nobody storms walls in the depths of winter. Technology, culture, politics and trade plans still update once a year. |
| **Population** | Growth and death rates differ by race. Famine, crowding, plague, disasters and war take their toll. Each race has a habitat bonus: dwarves can farm the mountains, elves the forests, lizardfolk the marshes. |
| **Markets and money** | Every town values each good by how much it needs it. Scarce, needed goods are worth more there (the town pays more for them and charges more to part with them); plentiful goods are cheap. Before a nation learns Currency its people **barter**: goods are paid for with goods of equal value, which is clumsier and moves less trade. After Currency, trade is paid in **coin** and spare gold is minted into it. The Charts tab shows the world's shift from barter to coin. |
| **Internal trade and roads** | **Markets:** hamlets and villages do not run markets of their own. Each trades through the nearest town of its own nation within about two days' travel (two and a half times the settlement spacing); where no town is near, the leading village of the cluster (the most developed, then the richest) holds the market for the rest. Villages sell their food and raw goods at market and buy crafts there; only markets trade with each other, send goods along a road to the nation's hub (its largest settlement), and send out merchant caravans. A settlement sells only what it can spare beyond its own needs and a safety reserve (for food, enough to eat until next year plus three months in store), and buys only what it is short of, so goods flow from where they are plentiful to where they are lacking rather than back and forth. Merchants' caravans follow the same rule, and goods a settlement buys and sells on in the same year are shown as passed on, not as its own trade. Tribute sent to the capital is counted apart from trade. This everyday trade, plus every caravan passing by, is traffic, and traffic builds **roads**. Feet wear trails for free. Above that, each nation spends from its treasury to upgrade its busiest stretches, as far as its knowledge allows: cart tracks with the Wheel, paved roads with Construction, highways with Engineering. Better roads make travel faster, so busy roads draw even more traffic, and neglected roads crumble. |
| **Free traders** | Caravans travel the map month by month to sell where their goods are dear. **Merchant caravans** set out from a town's markets. **Trading families** (named merchant houses such as "House Vel") grow richer and field bigger caravans with every venture, and can go bankrupt. **Nomad caravan tribes** from tribal and horse-riding peoples wander from market to market before returning home. How much a caravan carries depends on its size (pack animals, set by its town, house or tribe) and what it can get: horses to carry more, wagons once the Wheel is known. Caravans pay tolls at foreign markets and smaller tolls to each nation they pass through. They also trade a little in every town along the way, which enriches those towns and draws settlers. Enemies at war seize them, and bandits take them in the wilds. |
| **Trade agreements** | **Access:** each year every nation decides how it treats each neighbour's traders: *tolled* (welcome, but taxed), *passage only* (they may cross but not trade), or *closed*. Nations also **embargo** trade bound for their enemies. **Open Market Accords** between friendly, trade-minded nations remove the tolls. A nation shut out of a neighbour's lands may **ask for transit rights** for its caravans, which may be granted or refused. **Convoy compacts:** when free traders cannot bring in enough of something a nation needs (say iron), its rulers strike a state deal with a nation that has it to spare, such as "the Iron-for-Grain Compact": so much iron a year for so much grain, or for coin once both use currency. The goods travel by state convoy between the two hubs each year. The deals end when the need passes, relations sour, or war breaks out. |
| **Migration & colonisation** | People leave famine, overcrowding, plague and unrest for better places along trade routes, and prefer their own culture and kin. New settlements must keep a minimum distance from all others (the *settlement spacing*, 4 tiles by default). Crowded or expansionist settlements send colonists to the best reachable site for their race, overseas too once they can sail. The colonists set out as a visible **column of settlers** in spring and trek there over the following months, resting through winter. On arrival they found their settlement, or join the nearest town of their people if the land has been taken meanwhile. Colonies beyond a realm's reach become independent. |
| **Technology** | About 50 techs across eight eras, from Stone Age to Industrial. Knowledge grows out of **practice**: every technology a realm could take up next moves on each year according to how many of its people do the related work. Gatherers of wild grain and farmers find agriculture and better farming; hunters and herders tame animals and ride horses; fishers and traders learn to sail and navigate; quarrymen and townsfolk learn masonry and building; miners and smiths learn copper, bronze, iron and steel; traders, townsfolk and the business of governing many towns bring writing, currency, laws and banking; priests bring organised faith; war and weaponsmiths bring new ways of fighting. **Need** spurs invention: hunger speeds farming techniques, crowded towns medicine, sanitation and engineering, war military techs, a timber shortage masonry, a shortage of tools metalworking. Curious peoples, learned institutions, stable realms and wise rulers learn faster, and techs known by neighbours and trade partners are learned much faster. Some need resources: bronze needs copper *and* tin, gained through your own land or through trade. Some need water. The arcane branch exists only with magic on. Technology improves what people do and opens new kinds of work; it never decides how large a settlement can grow (see Development). |
| **Cultures** | Each culture has a language (its own sound palette for names) and eight values: militarism, mercantilism, piety, curiosity, xenophobia, tradition, seafaring and expansionism. Values adapt to how the people live. Cultures also develop **survival traits** from the environment they find themselves in: Seafarers, River-Tamers, Mountain Folk, Woodwise, Horse Lords, Sand-Walkers, Frostborn, Marsh-Dwellers, Great Hunters, Tillers of the Soil, Deep Delvers, Merchant Folk, Hardship-Hardened, Plague-Hardened and Warrior Tradition. Pressure from their land and way of life builds up over generations until the trait is earned (up to four at a time), and it fades if they leave that life behind. Traits make a people better at living off what they have: better yields, food from land others find barren, resistance to famine or disease, and stronger trade or arms. Adapted peoples also seek out more of the land they are adapted to when founding new settlements. Distant, isolated or overseas communities drift apart until a new daughter culture arises. Minorities assimilate into the ruling culture over time. |
| **Nations** | Tribes → chiefdoms → city-states, kingdoms, republics, theocracies and empires, depending on writing, size, diversity and values. Rulers have traits, age by their race's lifespan, and die, get assassinated or fall in battle, sometimes leaving a succession crisis. Stability depends on food, distance from the capital, culture, war weariness and overextension. Only deep, lasting discontent (very low stability *and* loyalty, not in a town's first years under a ruler) turns provinces to open revolt, and they found new realms. Small kin realms may join larger ones peacefully. |
| **Nobility** | Every nation past the tribal stage has a ruling **dynasty** (House such-and-such). As a nation grows, **great houses** rise in its chief towns, and every settlement is held as a **fief** by the nearest noble seat or directly by the crown. Houses gain prestige with their lands and grow loyal or restless with the ruler's character (just rulers are loved, cruel and weak ones are not), the nation's stability and wars, and their own ambition. Disloyal, ambitious houses **plot**: a strong house in a shaky realm may **seize the throne**; another may **renounce its oath** and break away with its fiefs as a new realm (often starting a civil war); a house on a hostile border may **defect** to the enemy with all its lands. When a ruler dies the heir usually comes from the dynasty; if the line fails, the strongest great house claims the throne or a new house is founded. Republics elect their First Consul from the great families. |
| **Loyalty & cohesion** | Each settlement has a **loyalty** to its nation, raised by stability, a shared culture, long rule and a loyal noble lord, and lowered by foreign rule, a rival with a claim who shares the townsfolk's culture, and above all by being **cut off**. Every two years each nation traces which of its towns it can still reach from its capital, over its own, allied, unclaimed or neutral land (never through enemies or closed borders), or by sea once it can sail. A cut-off town cannot trade with the rest of its nation or send it tribute. The longer it is cut off, the faster its loyalty drains. After six years a disloyal town (and its stranded neighbours) **opens its gates** to the power that surrounds it if it has any sympathy for it (kinsfolk, an old claim, strength), or **declares itself free**. Towns loyal enough hold out however long it takes. Low loyalty also feeds ordinary rebellions. |
| **Diplomacy** | Friendly neighbours arrange **royal marriages** between their ruling houses, and swear **defensive alliances** when a stronger power threatens both. Kin nations facing a common threat form **confederations** ("the Dushun League": one banner, many crowns). Members keep their own rulers and lands, but they stand together, trade freely and never fight one another. A beaten small realm may become a **vassal**, paying a yearly share of its treasury to its overlord. Restless vassals break free when strong enough, and loyal kin vassals are eventually folded peacefully into their overlord's realm. When a ruler bound by marriage dies without an heir, the crown may **pass to the allied dynasty and unite the two realms**. When war breaks out, the defender's allies, kin by marriage, confederates and overlord may answer the **call to arms** if they can reach the attacker (some ignore it), and an attacking overlord orders its border vassals in. Peace in the main war ends the allies' wars too. |
| **War aims** | Nations weigh what each enemy town is worth to them: its people and wealth; a **claim** from having held it before (claims fade over 150 years, and claimed towns poison relations with whoever holds them); kinsfolk living under foreign rule; goods the nation is short of and the town has to spare; towns wedged among its own, or standing between it and its cut-off lands; control of a trade road that caravans pass along, the rival's market hub, or a first harbour; and the enemy capital. Distance and fortifications count against it. The most valuable town becomes the war's **goal** and its cause in the chronicle ("over its claim to Saeian, lost in year 591"), and valuable targets make war more likely. Armies choose where to march by the same values, weighed against distance. A winning attacker may have the goal ceded at the peace table, and **peace terms** can impose vassalage or twenty years of tribute, sometimes sealed with a marriage. Towns won back from a former owner settle down faster than ones taken from strangers. |
| **War** | Relations reflect shared culture and race, trade agreements, marriages and alliances, claims, border friction, rival great powers and rulers' temperaments. How a nation fights follows history. Tribes, chiefdoms and anyone without bronze send out **warbands**, every able fighter. Kingdoms and city-states keep a small retinue and call out a **levy** of farmers in time of war (hunters serve as archers). Levies and warbands are raised each spring for the campaigning season and go home for the winter. Only with gunpowder (or, as with the Romans, the Warrior Code in an empire or republic) do nations keep **standing professional armies** in the field all year, and they fight better for it. When war breaks out, each side **raises named armies**, which move month by month (watch them in Real time) ("the Second Legion of X", "the First Warband of Y") at the settlement nearest the enemy. Armies march over the terrain, intercept enemy armies that come close (especially inside their own lands), fight named field battles ("the Battle of Kufiant Ford"), and besiege and storm towns. Terrain, walls, weapons, horses and warlike traits all count. Armies suffer attrition, worst in deserts, mountains, marshes and tundra, and every soldier lost is a person lost at home. A stormed town is **occupied**, not conquered: the occupier loots it and takes its taxes, and the owner's armies try to **liberate** it (an occupier's garrison is thin and the townsfolk help). Armies **occupy the land they march through**: during the war it shows striped in the occupier's colour inside a dotted line, and at the peace it goes to the winner (the dotted line becomes the new solid border) or back to its owner. Towns change hands only at the **peace**: the victor keeps the town it went to war for, towns it has a claim to, and a few of the most valuable others depending on how complete its victory was, and hands the rest back. A nation whose every town is occupied surrenders and is annexed. Occupied towns press their owner to make peace. Wars end in treaties followed by truces, and the armies go home. |
| **Events** | Regional droughts and bumper years, epidemics that ride the trade routes, earthquakes, floods, and (with magic) dragons, hydras and giants, sometimes slain by named heroes. Rich cities raise great works. The first people to master each tech and the first city to reach each size are recorded. |

## Positions on the land

The map has no visible grid. Every settlement stands at its own spot. When it is founded, it picks a place on its patch of land: a riverbank (never in the river), the shore just above the water, or level ground. The land it works and the realm around it are measured from that spot. Roads, trade routes, rivers, borders, and the paths of armies, caravans, settlers and wandering tribes are drawn as smooth curves. Travellers move along exactly the curve that is drawn. Roads wind between towns and meet at them.

Behind the scenes the simulation still keeps its books on a fine grid of small patches of land. Each patch has its own climate, soil and resources, an owner, and what it is used for. Movement costs and journeys are worked out on the same grid. None of this is drawn. What a settlement does with its land shows only in its Inspect panel.

**Borders.** Land is held by being there. A settlement holds the land its people work, and each year its hunters, herders and woodcutters range a little further into open land next to what it holds, a few tiles more as it grows, out to about three times the land it works. Clans claim land as their tribes walk it. Nothing is drawn in when a settlement is founded, and nothing fades. Where two holders' lands meet there is no wilderness between them, and only war moves that line. To draw the borders, each tile's holder is spread onto a three-times-finer, gently warped grid. Every pixel on screen then takes the holder with the most weight among the nearby cells, so each pixel belongs to exactly one holder. The border is simply where they meet: smooth, natural and never twisted. Borders between holders are solid. Land occupied in a war still being fought is striped with the occupier's colour inside a dotted line. The fills are clipped to the land, so they end exactly at the painted coast, and the layer is redrawn only when you pan or zoom or a border moves.

## Shaping the land

**Create world** in the top bar (or **Setup → Shape the land…**) opens the world editor. Shaping is done with one terrain tool. Drag on the map to use it. Right-drag or hold Space to pan, and scroll to zoom. `[` and `]` change the width, and Ctrl+Z undoes a stroke.

| Function | What it does |
|---|---|
| Raise | Lift the ground. Out of the sea it makes land. Keep going over it and it builds hills, then a mountain range. Drag along the line you want the range to follow. When you let go, running water carves it, leaving a sharp crest with spurs and valleys running down to the floor. |
| Lower | Sink the ground: cut valleys and passes, or drown land to make coves, inlets and seas. |
| Flatten | Level the ground to the height where the stroke began: plateaus, mesas and table lands, with steep edges. Raise a block first, then flatten its top. |

**Width** sets how wide the tool is. Use a wide tool for broad ranges and big landmasses, and a narrow one for thin, sharp ridges. **Strength** sets how much each pass lifts, sinks or levels the ground.

After every stroke the climate, rivers, lakes and biomes are rebuilt for the new land. The editor can show terrain, temperature, rainfall or currents, and it draws latitude lines every 15°. You can move the map on the globe or change the tilt and see the climate follow. Start from a blank ocean or freshly generated land. **Use this world** saves the land with the world's settings and starts a new history on it. The land is kept with the settings until you untick "Keep the hand-shaped land" in Setup. On a different map size, it is resampled to fit.

### Climate

- **Temperature** follows latitude, altitude and axial tilt. More tilt spreads the sun's warmth, so the poles are milder and the equator a little cooler.
- **Prevailing winds** follow the real three-cell pattern. Trade winds blow from the east below 30°, westerlies from the west between 30° and 60°, and polar easterlies above that. The winds carry rain inland and leave rain shadows behind mountains. The latitude belts add dry subtropics around 28°, a wet equator and a wet polar front near 60°.
- **Ocean currents** are traced as parcels of water. Each parcel runs with the wind until a coast stops it, then turns along the shore:
  - poleward in the tropics (warm currents like the Gulf Stream);
  - towards the equator between 30° and 48° (cold currents like the California Current);
  - poleward again up to 60° (the warm drift that keeps north-western coasts mild);
  - towards the equator under the polar easterlies (cold currents like the Labrador Current).

  Water keeps the temperature of the latitude it came from and loses it over about 25° of travel. A current carries on for a few degrees past where the wind turns, and the ocean wraps east to west. The warm or cold water warms or cools the sea and the coasts downwind, and it makes them wetter or drier: cold currents leave coastal deserts, warm ones bring rain.

## Project layout

```
src/engine/            deterministic simulation; no DOM, runs in Node or the browser
  world.ts             World state and the yearly tick
  worldgen.ts          terrain, latitude & climate, ocean currents, rivers, biomes, resources; height codes for hand-shaped land
  systems/landwars.ts  land wars involving clans, warbands, occupation of land and the peace that settles it
  erosion.ts           carving the land with running water: crests, spurs and valleys for the map's relief
  geometry.ts          continuous positions: settlement sites, river courses, waypoints, smooth paths
  editor.ts            the terrain tool (raise, lower, flatten) with undo
  data/                biomes, goods & production sectors, tech tree, races, culture traits (all plain data)
  systems/             territory, economy, trade, roads, caravans, access, agreements, migration, culture, politics, nobility,
                       diplomacy, cohesion, warAims, military, technology, events, setup
  chronicle.ts         Markdown chronicle, summaries, JSON snapshot
src/ui/                browser app: map renderer, smooth terrain painter, pixel-region borders, world editor, charts, panels, market, nation view, tech tree
src/cli.ts             headless runner
src/bench.ts           prints a 1000-year trajectory and timing (npx tsx src/bench.ts <seed> <years>)
tests/                 vitest suite (determinism, invariants, options, politics, exports)
```

A medium map (180×110) runs about 1,000 years in under a minute headless, with around 400 settlements, dozens of armies and hundreds of caravans over its history.

## Extending

Everything that defines a setting is data. Add a race in `src/engine/data/races.ts` (or in the Setup panel), add techs in `data/techs.ts` (prerequisites, resource requirements, effects), or new goods and production sectors in `data/economy.ts`. Systems are separate modules under `systems/`, each called once per year from `World.tick()`.
